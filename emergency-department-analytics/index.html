Healthcare reporting example

Emergency Department Analytics

An operational view of Emergency Department performance designed to move beyond headline targets and help explain when pressure builds, where patients are waiting and what may be slowing patient flow.

The situation

You know the department is under pressure. But where is it coming from?

St Anne’s Hospital is a fictional organisation created for this example, but the operational problem behind it is very real.

An Emergency Department produces a huge amount of information: attendances, arrival times, triage, waiting times, clinicians, admissions, discharges, presenting complaints and length of stay.

The headline performance measures are important, but they do not necessarily explain what is happening underneath them.

A drop in four-hour performance might coincide with higher demand. It might reflect a change in the type of patients attending. It might be linked to longer waits for assessment, increased admissions or patients spending longer in the department after a decision has been made.

The more useful question is:

Where is patient flow slowing, and what is contributing to it?

The question

The headline measure tells you there is pressure. It does not tell you where the pressure is.

Four-hour performance is a useful indicator, but it is an outcome of everything happening before that point.

Two days can produce the same headline result for completely different reasons.

One might have unusually high attendance. Another might have fewer patients but a greater proportion requiring admission or more complex assessment.

The same applies to average waiting times. An overall average can hide particular times of day, patient groups or stages of the pathway where delays are concentrated.

The reporting therefore needs to help teams move from:

Are we meeting the target?

to:

What is happening to patients as they move through the department?

The approach

Build the reporting around patient flow.

I built the report so the executive view provides the overall position, but the analysis does not stop there.

Attendances, four-hour performance, waiting time to clinician, length of stay, admissions, ambulance arrivals and patients leaving before being seen provide the starting point.

From there, the user can explore demand by hour and day, presenting complaints, arrival methods, triage, clinician workload and patient outcomes.

Those different views make it easier to distinguish between demand pressure and flow pressure rather than treating every deterioration in performance as the same problem.

The aim is not to fit every possible hospital measure onto a dashboard. It is to connect enough of the patient journey to make the operational conversation more useful.

What can you investigate?

Performance makes more sense when you can see what sits underneath it.

01

When does pressure build?

Look at attendance patterns by hour, day and month to understand when demand is highest and whether those patterns are consistent.

02

Where are patients waiting?

Compare waiting time to clinician, triage performance and length of stay to see where delays may be entering the patient journey.

03

Is the demand changing?

Explore arrival methods, presenting complaints, acuity and admission patterns to understand whether the type of activity is changing as well as the volume.

04

What happens to the patient afterwards?

Consider admissions, transfers, discharges and patients leaving before being seen alongside the headline performance measures.

Context matters

The same result can have very different causes.

This is one of the difficulties with operational healthcare reporting.

A single KPI can tell you that performance has changed without explaining why.

A busy Monday morning with a surge in walk-in attendances is different from a quieter period where a higher proportion of patients require admission and remain in the department for longer.

Both situations may result in deteriorating headline performance, but the operational response may be very different.

Good reporting does not make that judgement automatically. It gives clinical and operational teams enough context to ask the right question.

Reporting with context

Commentary that responds to what you are looking at.

The report also uses dynamic narrative to help describe the selected operational picture.

If a user moves from the whole department into a particular arrival method, clinician or patient population, the commentary changes with that selection.

That means the narrative can reflect the view being investigated rather than repeating the same summary regardless of what is currently on screen.

It does not replace the knowledge of the people running the department. It gives them a more useful starting point for interpreting what the data is showing.

What this is really about

Moving from monitoring performance to understanding flow.

This example is not really about building an Emergency Department dashboard.

It is about connecting the different stages of an operational process so that a headline measure can be understood in context.

Healthcare produces no shortage of data. The challenge is turning it into something that helps the people running a service understand what has changed, where pressure is developing and where they may need to look next.

The same principle applies beyond healthcare. Any service where work arrives, waits, moves through different stages and eventually leaves can benefit from looking at flow rather than isolated KPIs.

The target tells you how you performed. The flow helps explain why.
