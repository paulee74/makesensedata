Customer service reporting example

Customer Service & Contact Centre Analytics

An operational view designed to connect contact demand, responsiveness, resolution and customer outcomes so managers can understand not just how the service is performing, but what is driving the result.

The situation

Service levels have dropped. But why?

Harbour Customer Services is a fictional organisation created for this example, but the operational problem behind it is a familiar one.

A service operation can produce dozens of measures every day: contact volumes, answer rates, abandonment, speed of answer, handling time, resolution rates and customer satisfaction.

The difficulty is that those measures influence one another.

A fall in answer rate might be caused by a sudden increase in demand. It might reflect a change in channel mix, longer handling times or reduced capacity within a particular team.

And even if calls are being answered quickly, that does not necessarily mean customers are getting their issue resolved.

The more useful question is:

What is driving the change in service, and where should management look first?

The question

A KPI tells you what moved. It does not always tell you what caused it.

A contact centre might hit its answer-time target while customer satisfaction is falling.

Another team might have a longer average handling time but resolve more issues at the first contact.

A surge in one particular contact reason could create a very different operational problem from a general increase in demand across every channel.

Looking at each measure separately can therefore lead to the wrong conclusion.

The reporting needs to help managers move from:

Are we meeting the service level?

to:

What is happening to the customer journey underneath it?

The approach

Connect demand, service and customer outcome.

I built the report so the executive view provides the overall position, but the measures are not treated as separate scorecards.

Contact volume, answer rate, speed of answer, abandonment, handling time, first-contact resolution and customer satisfaction are viewed together.

From there, managers can move into individual teams, advisers, channels and contact reasons to understand what is contributing to the result.

Demand patterns can be compared with adviser workload and customer outcomes, making it easier to see whether a service issue is primarily about volume, capacity, complexity or resolution.

The aim is not to create a wall of contact-centre KPIs. It is to give managers enough context to understand what changed and decide where to investigate next.

Context matters

Faster is not always better.

This is one of the risks with operational service reporting.

Measures such as handling time and speed of answer are easy to track, but improving them in isolation does not necessarily improve the customer experience.

A shorter interaction may look efficient, but if the customer has to contact the organisation again the following day, the underlying issue has not really been resolved.

Equally, a team with longer handling times may be dealing with more complex work or resolving a higher proportion of cases at the first contact.

Good reporting should make those differences visible without pretending the numbers alone can explain every cause.

Demand and capacity

Sometimes the problem is performance. Sometimes it is simply too much work arriving at once.

One of the most useful distinctions in service operations is whether performance has changed because the operation is working differently or because demand has changed around it.

A demand heatmap can make recurring peaks visible. Channel and contact-reason analysis can show whether a particular type of work is increasing. Team and adviser views can then provide context around how that workload is being absorbed.

That changes the management conversation.

Instead of immediately asking why a team has missed a target, managers can first understand what arrived, when it arrived and whether the operation had the capacity to deal with it.

Reporting with context

Commentary that responds to what you are looking at.

The report also uses dynamic commentary to help describe the selected operational picture.

If a manager moves from the whole service into a particular team, adviser or channel, the narrative changes with that selection.

That allows the commentary to reflect the actual view being investigated rather than presenting the same management summary regardless of what is on screen.

It does not replace the judgement of the people running the service. It gives them a better starting point for deciding what deserves attention.

What this is really about

Moving from measuring service to understanding it.

This example is not really about building a contact-centre dashboard.

It is about connecting demand, capacity, responsiveness and customer outcome so that managers can understand what is changing within an operation.

Service teams rarely suffer from a lack of KPIs. The harder problem is working out which measures matter together and what they are actually saying about the customer experience.

The same approach can be applied to complaints, case management, service desks, claims, back-office operations and any environment where work arrives, gets handled and eventually reaches an outcome.

The KPI tells you what happened. Connecting the measures helps explain why.
