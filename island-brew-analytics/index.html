Retail & hospitality reporting example

Retail & Hospitality Analytics

A multi-site management view designed to look beyond sales and understand how margin, labour, waste and product mix are affecting the performance of each location.

The situation

The busiest location is not always the best-performing one.

Island Brew is a fictional Jersey coffee business created for this example, with several locations operating across the island.

At first glance, comparing the sites seems straightforward. Which one has the highest sales? Which one has the most transactions? Which one is ahead of target?

But those figures only tell part of the story.

A busy café might generate strong revenue while needing more staff to do it. Another may sell less but produce a better margin. Higher waste can quietly erode the benefit of increased sales, while a different product mix can make two apparently similar locations perform very differently.

The more useful question is:

Which locations are genuinely performing well, and what is driving the difference?

The question

Sales tell you how busy you are. They do not tell you how well you are running.

Looking at revenue alone can easily favour the largest or busiest location.

But a manager needs to know whether those sales are generating healthy margins, how much labour is required to produce them and whether waste is being kept under control.

Even apparently simple measures need some context. A higher average basket value may be positive, but is it coming from pricing, product mix or customers buying more items?

The same applies when one location appears to be underperforming. It may simply have lower footfall, or there may be something operational worth investigating.

The reporting therefore needs to help managers move from:

Which site sold the most?

to:

Which site is performing best, and why?

The approach

Put the commercial and operational picture together.

I built the report so sales are considered alongside the things that influence the quality of those sales.

Revenue, transactions, average basket value and gross margin provide the commercial picture. Labour, waste and target performance add the operational context.

Managers can start with the overall business, then move into a particular location, period or product category to understand why performance differs.

Because each site is measured using the same definitions, the comparisons become much more useful. A manager does not need a separate spreadsheet for each location or spend time trying to reconcile slightly different versions of the same measure.

The aim is to make it easier to spot a difference and then work out what sits behind it.

What can you investigate?

Performance looks different depending on what you measure.

01

Which locations are really performing best?

Compare sales, margin, targets, labour and waste rather than relying on a single measure to rank each site.

02

What is behind the sales figure?

Look at transaction volumes, average basket value and product mix to understand how revenue is being generated.

03

Is stronger revenue costing more to deliver?

Compare labour with sales and consider whether additional activity is translating into efficient performance.

04

Where is value being lost?

Bring waste into the same view to identify locations or periods where otherwise strong commercial performance may be getting eroded.

Context matters

A ranking tells you who is first. It does not tell you why.

Multi-site reporting can easily become a league table.

Location A has the most sales. Location B has the best margin. Location C has the lowest labour percentage.

Those comparisons are useful, but they need context.

Different sites may have different trading patterns, customer behaviour and product mixes. One location may be much busier at particular times of day. Another may sell a higher proportion of higher-margin products.

Good reporting should surface the difference without pretending the data alone explains everything.

It gives the manager somewhere sensible to investigate next.

One view of the business

Less time assembling reports. More time comparing what matters.

The practical benefit of bringing the information together is that commercial and operational performance no longer have to be reviewed separately.

A sales report does not need to sit in one spreadsheet while labour is maintained somewhere else and waste is reviewed in another process.

The same reporting model can provide the overall position and still allow individual managers to focus on their own location.

That creates one consistent starting point for the conversation, while still leaving room for the operational knowledge of the people actually running the sites.

What this is really about

Understanding the business behind the sales.

This example is not really about building a coffee shop dashboard.

It is about connecting different parts of an operation so that managers can understand performance in context.

The same principle applies to shops, restaurants, branches, venues or any other business operating across multiple locations.

When sales, margin, staffing and operational measures are considered together, the conversation changes from simply asking who sold the most to understanding why one part of the business is working differently from another.

Revenue tells you how much you sold. Good reporting helps explain how well you did it.
