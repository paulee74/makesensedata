Financial reporting example

Executive Financial Performance

A management view of financial performance designed to make it easier to move from the headline numbers into what is actually driving them.

The situation

The numbers exist. Getting to the answer is harder.

Harbour Engineering is a fictional multi-site business created for this example, but the reporting problem behind it is a very familiar one.

Finance has the actual results. Budgets are maintained separately. Customer, site and departmental information sit elsewhere. Each month those different pieces need to be brought together before management can get a complete view of performance.

That can mean a lot of effort goes into producing the reporting pack before anyone gets to the more useful part:

What changed, why did it change, and is there anything we need to do about it?

The question

A headline figure tells you what happened.

Revenue being ahead of budget is useful to know, but it immediately creates another set of questions.

Which part of the business is ahead? Is the improvement coming from more customers, larger orders or a particular site? Is the additional revenue translating into better profitability?

The same applies when performance deteriorates. A fall in margin at organisation level does not tell management whether the issue is widespread or concentrated in one part of the business.

The reporting therefore needs to help people move naturally from:

What happened?

to:

Why did it happen?

The approach

Build the reporting around the management conversation.

I built the report so the first view gives management the overall financial position: revenue, profitability, margins and performance against budget.

From there, the user can follow the numbers into individual sites, departments, customers and revenue streams without needing another spreadsheet or a separate analysis.

Actual and budget information sit within the same reporting model, so variance analysis becomes part of the normal reporting process rather than something that has to be recreated each month.

The aim is not to put every available financial measure on a screen. It is to show enough to spot something that needs attention, then make it straightforward to investigate it.

What can you investigate?

The useful questions sit underneath the headline.

01

What has changed?

Compare revenue, profitability, margins and previous periods to understand how the overall financial position is moving.

02

Where is the budget variance coming from?

Move from the organisation-wide result into individual sites and departments to see where actual performance differs from plan.

03

What is driving revenue?

Explore customers, sectors, locations and order values to understand where changes in revenue are actually coming from.

04

Is growth translating into profit?

Look beyond sales alone and consider gross profit, operating profit and margins alongside revenue performance.

Reporting with context

Commentary that changes with the question.

One part of the example I particularly wanted to explore was management commentary.

Traditional reporting often combines charts and tables with commentary that has to be rewritten manually each month. It can also mean that the narrative describes the organisation as a whole even when somebody is looking at a particular site or department.

In this report, narrative measures respond to the filters and selections being made. Someone reviewing a particular area of the business therefore sees commentary relating to that view.

It does not replace financial judgement. It gives the person making that judgement a better starting point.

Under the bonnet

The technology supports the reporting.

01

Structured data model

General ledger, sales, budget, customer, site, department and date information are connected through a structured analytical model.

02

Financial measures

Revenue, gross profit, operating profit, EBITDA, margins, year-on-year growth and budget variance are calculated dynamically using DAX.

03

Customer analysis

Customer ranking, active customer counts, average order value and concentration measures help show where revenue is coming from.

04

Dynamic narrative

Commentary measures respond to the current filters and provide contextual explanations alongside the financial results.

What this is really about

Changing the balance of effort.

This example is not really about creating a finance dashboard.

It is about reducing the amount of effort required to gather information, reconcile spreadsheets, rebuild reporting packs and answer the same follow-up questions each month.

A well-structured reporting model gives people one consistent starting point and lets them explore the information themselves when something needs further investigation.

Less time producing the numbers. More time understanding what they mean.
