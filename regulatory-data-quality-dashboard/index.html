Regulatory data quality example

Regulatory Data Quality Dashboard

A reporting model designed to move beyond counting data quality exceptions and help teams understand which issues matter most, what they affect and where remediation effort should be focused.

The situation

You have thousands of issues. Where do you start?

This example represents a common regulatory data quality problem: an organisation knows that exceptions exist, but the exception count alone does not tell management how concerned they should be.

One rule might produce a large number of relatively minor issues. Another might affect far fewer records but relate to an important regulatory obligation or a higher-risk population.

Treating every exception equally can mean remediation effort gets spread across a long list of problems without enough consideration of their relative significance.

The more useful question is:

Which issues matter most, what do they affect and what should we address first?

The question

An exception count tells you the size of the list. It does not tell you the size of the risk.

Data quality reporting often begins with volume: how many records failed a rule, how many issues are open or how many customers are affected.

Those numbers are useful, but they can be misleading if they are viewed without context.

A rule producing 5,000 exceptions is not automatically more important than one producing 50.

The significance depends on what the rule is checking, which regulatory requirement it supports, who or what is affected and how serious the failure could be.

The reporting therefore needs to help teams move from:

How many issues do we have?

to:

Which issues create the greatest concern?

The approach

Put the issue in context.

I built the model so that data quality exceptions are not considered in isolation.

Each issue can be connected to the rule that identified it, the regulatory obligation that rule supports and information about its relative severity and impact.

That makes it possible to look at exception volume alongside significance rather than assuming the biggest number should always receive the most attention.

Users can start with the overall position and then move into individual obligations, rules, client classifications, entity types or booking centres to understand where exposure is concentrated.

The aim is not to turn risk into a single magic score. It is to give teams a more useful basis for deciding where to look first.

Context matters

The biggest problem is not always the biggest number.

This is one of the areas where data quality reporting can become misleading if everything is reduced to a simple count.

A high-volume issue may be operationally inconvenient but relatively straightforward to manage. A much smaller set of exceptions may affect a key regulatory field, a particular client population or an important reporting obligation.

That does not mean a scoring model can make the remediation decision automatically.

Severity and impact still require judgement, and the business context matters.

What the reporting can do is bring those different pieces together so that the discussion starts with more than a spreadsheet full of failed records.

From issue to action

Data quality reporting becomes useful when it helps someone decide what to do next.

Finding an exception is only the beginning of the process.

Someone still needs to understand the cause, determine whether the issue is isolated or systemic, decide who owns the fix and work out how urgently it needs to be addressed.

Bringing rules, obligations, affected populations and relative impact into the same reporting model creates a much better starting point for those conversations.

It also gives governance teams a consistent way to monitor the position as remediation work progresses, rather than rebuilding the analysis for each review meeting.

What this is really about

Turning a list of problems into a set of priorities.

This example is not really about building a regulatory dashboard.

It is about taking a large and potentially overwhelming population of data quality issues and adding enough context to make them manageable.

When exception volume, business rules, regulatory obligations and relative impact are connected, teams can spend less time debating which spreadsheet to look at and more time deciding which problems actually need to be fixed.

The same principle can be applied well beyond regulatory reporting: control failures, reconciliation breaks, overdue actions, operational exceptions and other areas where the sheer number of issues can make prioritisation difficult.

Knowing how many issues you have is useful. Knowing which ones matter is much more useful.
