Automation

The cost of manual reporting.

Copying, pasting and rebuilding the same report every week doesn't just consume time. It creates an unnecessary dependency on people remembering exactly how the process works.

Many organisations have reports that work perfectly well.

Every Monday somebody downloads a spreadsheet. They copy some data into another spreadsheet. A few formulas update. Several tables are refreshed. Charts are copied into a PowerPoint presentation.

The finished report is emailed to management.

Nothing about that process necessarily looks broken — until you ask how much work goes into producing it.

"It only takes a couple of hours"

Two hours a week doesn't sound particularly significant.

But over a year that's roughly 100 hours spent producing essentially the same report.

2 hours every week ≈ 100 hours a year

And the visible production time isn't always the whole cost.

There may also be time spent checking the numbers, correcting formatting, finding missing files, updating formulas, answering questions about why this month's figure doesn't match somebody else's, and fixing the report when the person who normally produces it is away.

The report gets produced, so the process survives. That doesn't mean the process is efficient.

Manual processes create risk

The more steps somebody has to perform manually, the more opportunities there are for something to go wrong.

  • A formula isn't copied far enough.
  • The wrong month's file is selected.
  • A column moves.
  • A filter remains active.
  • Somebody overwrites a value.

The problem with many spreadsheet errors isn't that spreadsheets are inherently bad.

It's that complicated reporting processes can depend on a sequence of actions that exists largely in one person's head.

The organisation isn't just dependent on the spreadsheet. It's dependent on the person who knows how the spreadsheet works.

Automation doesn't mean removing people

The objective isn't to automate everything simply because we can.

Some parts of reporting genuinely require judgement. People need to interpret results, understand context and decide what action to take.

Those are exactly the things we should be spending time on.

What we can reduce is the repetitive work required before somebody gets to that point.

  • Importing files.
  • Combining datasets.
  • Applying the same transformations.
  • Recalculating measures.
  • Rebuilding charts.
  • Refreshing standard reports.

Those are good candidates for automation because the rules are already known.

Start with the repetitive part

Reporting automation doesn't necessarily require a huge technology project.

A useful starting point is simply to map the existing process.

  • Where does the data come from?
  • What happens to it?
  • Which steps are repeated every time?
  • Which steps require judgement?
  • Where are errors most likely?
  • Where does the process regularly get stuck?

Once that's understood, individual pieces can often be simplified or automated without replacing every system involved.

Create a repeatable flow

Ideally, reporting begins to look less like this:

Download → Copy → Paste → Manipulate → Check → Rebuild → Send

and more like this:

Update data → Refresh → Review → Act

The underlying work hasn't disappeared. It has been moved into a repeatable process where the same rules are applied consistently every time.

That improves efficiency, but it also improves confidence in the result.

Use people's time for the useful part

I don't think the main benefit of reporting automation is simply saving a few hours.

It's what those hours are currently being spent doing.

A skilled analyst shouldn't need to spend Monday morning copying numbers between spreadsheets just so they can begin analysing them on Monday afternoon.

Once repetitive production work is reduced, people can spend more time asking:

  • Why has this changed?
  • Is this expected?
  • What should we investigate?
  • What happens next?

That's where reporting starts becoming genuinely useful.
