Why dashboards miss the real question.

A dashboard can contain perfectly accurate data and still fail to tell anyone what they need to know.

There is a temptation when building a dashboard to start with the data.

What fields have we got? What measures can we calculate? What charts could we build?

That can produce an impressive-looking dashboard surprisingly quickly. It can also produce something nobody actually uses.

I prefer to start somewhere else.

Start here What decision are we trying to make?

A dashboard isn't the objective

Nobody really needs a dashboard.

They need to understand something.

A finance director might want to know why costs have increased. A sales manager might want to know which opportunities are most likely to convert. An operational team might need to know where a service is beginning to struggle.

Management might simply want one reliable version of a number rather than three spreadsheets containing three different answers.

The dashboard is just the mechanism for making that information easier to understand.

That distinction matters.

More information isn't necessarily better

One of the easiest mistakes to make in reporting is trying to include everything.

If the data exists, somebody asks for it to be added. Eventually the report contains dozens of KPIs, charts, filters and tables.

Technically, it may be very comprehensive. Practically, the important information becomes harder to see.

A useful dashboard should establish a hierarchy.

  • What do I need to know first?
  • What looks unusual?
  • What has changed?
  • Where should I investigate?

The detailed information can still be available, but it doesn't necessarily need to compete for attention on the first screen.

Start with the questions

Before building anything, I like to understand the questions people are repeatedly trying to answer.

That often means talking to the people who currently produce and use the reports.

  • What do you look at every morning?
  • What gets discussed at the management meeting?
  • Which number causes arguments?
  • What do people regularly ask you to investigate?
  • What takes too long to find?

Those conversations are often more useful than starting with a list of available database fields.

They tell you what the reporting actually needs to do.

One number should mean one thing

Another common problem appears when different parts of an organisation calculate the same measure differently.

Finance has one version. Operations has another. Someone has a spreadsheet they've been maintaining for three years.

All of them are described as the same KPI.

At that point, the problem isn't visualisation. It's definition.

Before building the dashboard, the organisation needs to agree what the measure actually means and how it should be calculated.

A good reporting solution makes those definitions consistent and repeatable. That can be considerably more valuable than the charts themselves.

The best dashboards lead somewhere

A useful report should allow somebody to move naturally from:

What happened? → Why did it happen? → What should we do about it?

The first page might therefore be deliberately simple: a few important measures, trends, exceptions and areas requiring attention.

From there, users can explore the detail when they need it.

The result is often a dashboard containing less information on screen, not more. But every element has a reason for being there.

Technology comes later

Power BI is an excellent tool for presenting and exploring information. But Power BI doesn't know which question matters to your organisation.

That's the part that comes first.

Understand the question. Agree the definitions. Get the underlying data into a reliable structure. Then build the reporting around the decisions people actually need to make.

The dashboard is the final layer, not the starting point.
