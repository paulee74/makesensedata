Regulatory reporting

Gender pay gap reporting: the numbers are only the starting point.

Producing the calculation is one thing. Understanding what is driving the result is where the useful work begins.

Gender pay gap reporting can easily become an exercise in producing a handful of headline percentages.

Mean pay gap. Median pay gap. Bonus gap. Pay quartiles.

Those measures are important, but on their own they don't tell you very much about what is actually happening within an organisation.

A 20% pay gap doesn't mean women are being paid 20% less than men for doing the same job. Equally, calculating the figure correctly doesn't mean there isn't something worth investigating.

The useful question What is driving the gap?

Start with the workforce, not the percentage

One of the first things I would look at is how employees are distributed across the organisation.

Are men and women represented equally at different levels of seniority? Is one gender concentrated in particular departments? What does the distribution look like across pay quartiles? Are there differences in length of service, working patterns or job grades?

A headline figure can often be the result of several structural factors working together.

For example, an organisation might have a workforce that is predominantly female overall but still have more men occupying its highest-paid positions.

The overall workforce split could therefore look relatively balanced while the pay gap tells a very different story.

That's why I wouldn't look at the headline number in isolation.

Mean and median tell different stories

The mean is affected by particularly high or low salaries. The median looks at the employee in the middle of the pay distribution.

Looking at both gives a better indication of the shape of the workforce. A large difference between the two can itself be useful information.

Rather than simply putting both percentages into a report, I'd want to understand why they differ. That means being able to move from the headline measures into the underlying workforce data.

Pay quartiles are particularly useful

Splitting employees into four equally sized pay groups provides a simple way of seeing where men and women sit within the organisation's pay distribution.

If women make up a large proportion of the lower quartile but a much smaller proportion of the upper quartile, that tells us something the overall gender split cannot.

It doesn't provide the answer by itself. But it gives us somewhere useful to investigate.

The same principle applies to job grade, department, location and other organisational dimensions.

Reporting should help people ask the next question

This is where good reporting becomes much more valuable than simply producing a statutory figure once a year.

Instead of a spreadsheet containing a set of calculations, the organisation can have a repeatable reporting process that allows it to explore the result.

A dashboard might show the headline measures but also allow users to examine:

  • gender representation by pay quartile;
  • representation by grade or seniority;
  • bonus participation;
  • departmental differences;
  • changes between reporting periods.

The objective isn't to produce more charts. It's to make it easier to understand what is behind the numbers.

Build a process you can repeat

Another consideration is what happens next year.

If producing the report requires somebody to extract several spreadsheets, clean them manually, combine them and recreate the same calculations every year, there is an opportunity to improve the process.

The underlying data model and calculations can be designed once and reused. New data can then be added to the same reporting structure, making it easier to compare periods and identify changes.

That turns gender pay gap reporting from an annual calculation into something much more useful:

A way of understanding how the shape of the workforce is changing over time.
